Finding Search Decay Before the Refresh Queue

A directional decision-support study for prioritizing editorial refreshes from observed search visibility symptoms.

FlyRank ML InternshipAugust 2026Random Forest + time-aware evaluation

Title + Abstract

This study asks whether historical search visibility metrics can identify content showing search traffic decay early enough to prioritize editorial refreshes. It uses the fact_content_daily_performance table from the FlyRank warehouse through DuckDB on Hugging Face, with March 2026 for training and the June 2026 final-month sample for testing. A Random Forest classifier was compared with a fixed Week 4 heuristic using the needs_refresh proxy label, defined as zero clicks with positive impressions. In the archived Week 5 comparison, the Random Forest measured 99.97% precision, 100.00% recall, and 99.99% accuracy versus 12.89%, 0.44%, and 61.89% for the heuristic on the same June split. The resulting probability-ranked playbook is intended to help editors review likely decay symptoms in order, not to automate publishing or claim causal traffic recovery.

Introduction / Problem Statement

Editorial teams cannot refresh every page at once. This decision-support tool identifies pages exhibiting decaying organic-search symptoms so an editor can prioritize a refresh queue rather than guess from a large inventory.

The output is a ranked review list. A wrong positive costs editorial time; a wrong negative may leave a page's search opportunity unattended. The useful question is therefore operational: can measured historical patterns make review order more consistent?

Data

The analysis uses the fact_content_daily_performance table from the FlyRank warehouse, queried with DuckDB against the gated Hugging Face release. The grain is daily performance by report date, pseudonymous client, and pseudonymous content item.

The split is time-aware: month=2026-03 is used for training, while the June 2026 final-month _sample is reserved exclusively for testing. Mathematically derived fields such as ctr, label-derived fields, and future metrics are excluded from the requested capstone feature contract to reduce leakage risk. Pseudonymous IDs are grouping keys only.

Methodology

The target is needs_refresh = (clicks = 0 AND impressions > 0). The requested feature set is impressions, position, and is_weekend. A Random Forest classifier combines many shallow thresholding trees, allowing non-linear interactions between visibility, rank, and calendar context while retaining feature-importance diagnostics.

The Week 4 baseline is intentionally rigid: flag a page when position <= 10, impressions > 1000, and ctr < 0.02. It is transparent and useful as a decision benchmark, but it cannot adapt its boundaries to combinations of signals.

Reproducibility note. The archived w05_model.ipynb run used impressions, ctr, and is_weekend because its query could not resolve position. The requested position-based contract is recorded here, but the archived scores below should be rerun with that contract before being treated as a final benchmark.

The measured difference is consistent with the model learning non-linear relationships among the available metrics instead of applying one hard boundary. In practical terms, the archived run produced far fewer false positives while retaining the observed positive cases. These are evaluation results for a proxy label, not evidence that refreshing a page causes traffic to recover.

Limitations

The model only detects symptoms of search traffic drops in the measured search fields. It remains blind to traffic from social media, direct links, and email. It also cannot identify purely informational “zero-click” search intents, where a searcher may receive the answer without clicking a page.

These results are observed and directional. They do not predict Google's algorithm, prove content decay as a cause, or guarantee that an editorial refresh will recover traffic. Human review remains necessary.
